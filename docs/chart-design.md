# Chart design fundamentals for autonomous agents

## Purpose and authority

Build musical phrases that are coherent in time, space, player action, and visual presentation. Apply these fundamentals during ordinary charting without waiting for the user to request each one. They apply to static judgment lines and ordinary straight note approach as well as animated passages.

The user accepted these principles as the baseline design direction on 2026-11-02. This establishes a local design standard, not a claim that every official chart follows it. Individual arrangements, emotional interpretations, and comfort predictions still require evidence and review.

Read this document alongside [AGENTS.md](AGENTS.md) and the current song's project records. Those instructions govern live editing, scope, timing, fingering, verification, and sample review. Apply the principles within the authorized task; they do not authorize redesigning unrelated passages or changing approved timing.

## Core design requirement

A chart should communicate recognizable musical phrases through its playable arrangement. Accurate timestamps are necessary, but independent note placements do not establish a phrase. Spatial relationships, repetition, grouping, attention, and transitions must work together.

Throughout the chart means consistent attention to these concerns. It does not mean constant motion, constant novelty, or using every technique in every passage. A stationary anchor, repeated position, sparse passage, or static line can be the correct choice.

Keep three layers distinct:

| Layer | Design responsibility |
| --- | --- |
| Player action and judgment positions | What the player hits or maintains contact with, when, and how successive actions connect. |
| Note approach | How notes become visible and travel toward those judgment positions. Ordinary straight approach is the default. |
| Background-line choreography | Decorative visual support with its own explicit musical timing and subordinate role. It is optional. |

Apparent note travel does not establish finger travel. Additional approach animation does not repair an incoherent arrangement.

## Phrase design principles

### 1. Compose spatial phrases

Choose a coherent spatial relationship for each action phrase: progression, return, expansion, contraction, an anchor with responses, or another musically supported structure. Place notes as members of that phrase rather than choosing each location independently.

For an alternating eight-hit passage, inspect each hand's successive targets and the whole group's visible shape. Adjacent opposite-hand notes can be close while each hand travels substantially across its own successive hits. Conversely, alternating left and right at two fixed points does not create broad travel.

Check whether the phrase's spatial behavior can be described concretely and whether the actual judgment positions support that description. Do not require broad travel in every style or maximize distance for its own sake.

### 2. Give recurring musical ideas a recognizable identity

When a musical figure recurs, preserve a recognizable relationship in its grouping, hand actions, spatial contour, or anchor structure. Exact repetition is valid. If the music develops, a mirror, translation, expansion, role exchange, or changed ending may express that development.

State what remains recognizable and what changes. Avoid replacing the arrangement on every repetition solely to create variety. Also avoid copying a pattern mechanically when the music, preceding hand state, or next phrase changes its requirements.

### Guidance: intended hands through progressive repetition

The user calls this **引导 / Guidance**: arrange the player's preceding experience
so that the intended hand choice becomes understandable before a less obvious
layout arrives. Apply it when authoring phrases that depend on a particular hand
allocation. A hand label in an action ledger is an intention, not player guidance.
Repetition with gradual spatial development is one useful mechanism, not the
only mechanism or a requirement for every passage.

The user's example uses positions1–4 ranked from left to right, and a four-note
sequence in time:

| Stage | Temporal hit order | Intended hands | What establishes or preserves the reading |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Establish | 1,3,2,4 | L,R,L,R | L takes positions1/2 on the left; R takes3/4 on the right. Spatial placement makes the alternating allocation intuitive. |
| Develop over repetitions | Move the second temporal note leftward and the third rightward, toward and then through the middle | L,R,L,R | Preserve recognizable rhythm, grouping and outer anchors while those two targets shift incrementally. Track note roles through the repetitions rather than reassigning hands from screen halves. |
| Arrive | 1,2,3,4 | L,R,L,R | The intended paths are now L:1→3 and R:2→4. Prior repetitions supply the hand-choice context that an isolated final cell would lack. |

Numbers denote spatial order, not immutable note IDs. The second and third
temporal note roles persist as their spatial ranking changes. This is successive
repetition of changed landing positions, not a request for sideways note animation
during approach. No exact coordinates, repetition count, subdivision or transition
duration are prescribed by the example.

For a guidance-dependent phrase, record the intuitive initial reading, what
remains recognizable, what changes on each recurrence, and how the final reading
connects to the next phrase. Evaluate the complete visible and playable sequence:
does the established hand order survive rests, incoming notes, contacts, carrier
changes and section joins? Avoid simultaneously changing rhythm, spatial roles
and presentation so extensively that the earlier reading no longer helps. Keep
the changes consistent with the actual musical repetitions; do not add arbitrary
notes or repeats solely to teach an unmotivated pattern.

Guidance makes a route more legible; it does not enforce a finger choice, erase
physical difficulty or establish that the player followed the intended route.
Compare plausible alternative readings, especially near the middle and after
breaks, and retain human review. Do not treat this illustrative1,3,2,4→1,2,3,4
progression as a mandatory template or as permission to copy the approved silent
reference's cells.

### 3. Design entries and exits

Evaluate a phrase together with the end of the preceding phrase and the beginning of the following one. Account for hand positions, occupied contacts, releases, available travel time, incoming notes, and the visual focus of the screen.

A good isolated pattern can still produce a poor transition. Check for accidental same-hand fresh-hit repetitions, abrupt relocations, incomplete contact gestures, and unrelated changes of visual direction at joins. Deliberate discontinuity needs a concrete musical purpose and readable execution; smoothness is not a reason to erase a real musical break.

### 4. Establish visual priority

Make the current primary task legible: for example, alternating hits, a sustained contact with responses, synchronized accents, or a move to another screen region. Organize supporting elements around that task.

Use restraint when several elements compete for attention. Simultaneous gestures can be appropriate, but their relationship should be understandable. Neither permanent symmetry nor equal activity on every line is required. Judge hierarchy in the moving scene, including notes approaching future judgments, rather than from a single attractive frame.

### 5. Use spacing and silence deliberately

Treat compactness, spread, occupied screen area, and empty regions as composition choices. Preserve musical rests and allow gestures to finish. A sparse phrase need not cover the whole screen; a quiet moment need not acquire decoration to remain visually occupied.

Inspect whether spacing clarifies grouping and supports the intended hand paths. Do not increase note density, add drags, or enlarge every movement merely to fill space. A rest can also provide a meaningful new entry or hand-role reset; retain its actual duration when evaluating continuity.

### 6. Align visual grouping with musical grouping

Use position, spacing, recurring shapes, simultaneous relationships, and action continuity to make the intended musical groups readable. For a phrase heard as three notes, three notes, and a two-note ending, avoid accidentally presenting an unrelated four-plus-four structure.

Establish the grouping from the actual passage. Do not infer it solely from a convenient grid or force every phrase into a fixed length. Preserve accepted timing while refining spatial grouping. Pitch-to-position mapping is an optional expressive choice, not a universal requirement.

### 7. Develop contrast over the whole chart

For a requested wide-travel/performance chart, contrast must survive inspection of
the actual action sequences and rendered movement. The user rejected run02's
slowly interpolated left/right lanes, sparse background accents and occasional
receiver dips. Compose articulated per-hit excursions and recognizable cells in
wide-travel passages; provide contrasting contact/anchor/response grammars elsewhere.
Keep background choreography and beat-driven receiver response sustained through
their designated musical phrases. Do not substitute isolated accent events or
section names for these concrete outcomes. Preserve intentional quiet passages,
but do not let generic restraint erase the requested performance density.

Plan how recurring sections relate and where the chart's spatial and visual intensity develops or relaxes. Compare action density, travel span, compactness, visual activity, and recovery opportunities separately; they are not interchangeable measures of intensity.

A climax does not automatically need more lines or larger rotations. A restrained passage can make a later spatial expansion meaningful, while recurring material should retain enough identity to be recognized. Choose the variables that serve the current music and difficulty target. Do not introduce changes simply because a section label says "chorus" or "drop."

### 8. Maintain readability and action continuity

Treat cleanup as a routine part of authoring. Check unintended occlusion, ambiguous simultaneous relationships, sudden judgment-position changes, and line resets or reuse that disturb notes still visible. Review full visible note lifetimes and hold tails whenever carrier motion is involved.

Evaluate plausible fingerings using actual screen-space judgments and contact requirements. A convincing autoplay video does not establish comfortable execution. Preserve unresolved assumptions in the project record instead of labeling an untested passage comfortable or user-approved.

## Baseline movement and effect cadence

For an ordinary performance chart, give every section a legible receiving surface or a readable landing region, a recognizable direction of approach, and a relationship between the movement and the musical phrase. Describe what players see between the accents: where the receiver settles, what advances or recedes, which layer holds the eye, and how the next action enters. A hidden carrier can still deliver notes to a clear location. An explicitly requested pure-configuration chart follows its single-line brief.

**Set a baseline for each section.** A steady groove can use a mostly horizontal receiver with a small displacement and recovery on the recurring accent. An opening with exposed sustains can let the contact path carry the movement while the background holds. In a designated dance passage, carry the chosen response through the whole figure: open, travel, arrive, recover, then answer or repeat on the next musical anchor. Develop the identity on a return by changing direction, span, emphasis or the line that answers. Leave enough visual separation for the player to follow the receiving notes.

Official references show how often ordinary movement coexists with familiar arrivals. Across 344 charts, the median chart had a line within 10° of horizontal at 95% of note heads. Visible stroke travel or rotation appeared in 85% of half-second windows; including opacity changes raised this to 91%. On wholly empty lines the median motion coverage was 39%. Thus readable landings and an active scene can coexist. A permanent decorative pair is not required. See the [whole-chart evidence, definitions and timelines](../../research/judgment-line-study/cadence.html).

**Plan three timescales separately.**

These figures are descriptive results from the linked screen-space detectors. They do not identify instrument parts or count semantic changes of motif. Use a reference passage at a comparable density to check the draft, then judge the draft with its own audio. A long freeze during an active rhythmic phrase deserves review, as does an ensemble that makes the same motion throughout contrasting sections. A quiet section can earn its stillness. In the song record, state **what persists, what repeats on which sound, and what changes at the phrase boundary**. Keep the source render or chart time beside any reference used.

### Compose line performance from a musical phrase

Check its account against the actual audio, chart offset and beat map. Mark the phrase start, recurring attacks, rests and release in target time. Identify the rhythm the player taps and the layer the background follows. Describe the articulation in audible terms such as a sharp attack followed by a long decay. 2. **Query the bank for the visual job.** Use the category `background` for scene motion or `note` for a changed approach. ### Calibrate scroll and movement against a short phrase

When a player reports crowded, slow arrivals and stiff line movement, test those judgments independently. Freeze the actual timed note arrangement. Measure visible approach time and simultaneous on-screen heads in the dense passage, and find a comparable reference with a similar local onset rate and receiving geometry. For motion, specify the musical contact, the time and extent of the depression, the recovery, and the handoff into the next figure. Verify rendered positions through the phrase.

Produce matched native clips when multiple changes could explain an improvement. Compare original and faster scroll with the same choreography, then compare movement alternatives at the same speed. Record which property the user prefers before carrying settings into a full chart. ## Choosing animation and emotional expression

Start with the musical and action phrase, then decide whether an exceptional note approach or decorative line effect adds a specific contribution. Do not add habitual sine drift, sideways entrances, swaying, or settle-before-hit animation as a default polish pass. A static line with deliberate grouping and landing positions can already communicate strong visual flow.

The [judgment-line bank](../../research/judgment-line-study/index.html) supplies mechanisms, examples, and implementation evidence. It does not supply a validated emotion-to-pattern mapping. Reusing an effect from an official chart does not establish its suitability for a new song.

Before selecting an effect, identify the audible feature it supports and the visual behavior intended to express it. For example, a sustained phrase might motivate a continuous expansion, whereas a sequence of discrete accents might motivate repeated changes. These are candidate interpretations, not fixed rules such as "sustained sound means expansion." Explain the choice using the actual passage, including why it supports the playable layer.

**Background-line movement must be 100% on rhythm.** Follow the detailed rule in [AGENTS.md](AGENTS.md#background-line-choreography-and-musical-rhythm): identify the driving musical layer and align perceptual motion accents to its events. Repeating drum figures generally call for consistent intervals and phase; follow actual rests, syncopations, fills, and tempo changes. Melody-driven decoration is valid but less common because playable notes usually already express the melody. Smooth movement between musical anchors is allowed.

If the musical basis for decoration is uncertain, keep it static or omit the effect. Continue supported arrangement work within scope. Do not invent musical evidence or confuse rhythmic correctness with emotional suitability.

## Playable-line readability and rhythmic articulation

The user rejected the first Happy ending run for leaving idle carriers visible,
confusing which line receives notes, and decorative movement that felt slow,
stale and off rhythm. Treat that feedback as a failed presentation result, not
as a passed design merely because validation and source timestamps matched.

Schedule carrier visibility around note approach and sustained contact. A clear primary receiver may remain visible through note-free intervals;
recede when needed to avoid ambiguous competing receivers. Reveal the receiving
carrier before players need to associate approaching notes with it. Decoration should not create competing
judgment surfaces. A modest rhythmic translation of the active line is welcome,
subject to actual landing/contact displacement and readable transitions.

Design each movement's time profile, including the perceptible accent and its
recovery. A named easing curve or large event count does not prove acceleration,
phrasing, or synchronization is perceptually clear. Do not use unsupported drum
labels: identify real rhythmic evidence before designing drum-driven pulses.

The user specifically welcomes a playable line that appears to be struck downward
by arriving notes and then rebounds. Design the contact, depression and recovery
as one perceptible response anchored to selected musical actions. Check the
resulting moving judgments and sustained contacts; do not let dense independent
pulses collide or substitute an arbitrary up/down loop for the musical phrase.

### Receiver identity through orientation

The user rejected the first climax diagnostics as still very poor, while ranking
C relatively best. Its receiving-plane change remained ambiguous. When two
plausible receiving lines coexist, distinguish their orientations: for example,
tilt the incoming active receiver one way and the previous receiver the opposite
way. The notes' visible orientation should match their own receiving line, so the
player can identify the destination before judgment. Opacity differences alone
do not establish that association. Check both directions of the handoff and the
entire overlapping approach window; do not let a background stroke mimic the
same receiving orientation. This is a readability mechanism for competing
surfaces, not a requirement that every line in every scene constantly tilts.

## Autonomous authoring procedure

1. **Read the current context.** Read the song records and fresh live passage. Identify the authorized range, accepted timing, difficulty target, relevant user preferences, and any existing motif to preserve.
2. **Identify the musical phrase.** Locate its actual rhythmic groups, rests, accents, repetition, and changes. Separate audible evidence from inferred instrument identity or emotional interpretation.
3. **Design the player actions and spatial phrase.** Assign candidate hands and contact states, choose the grouping and spatial relationship, and resolve entry and exit. Use the detailed action rules in AGENTS.md.
4. **Establish visual priority.** Check spacing, visible overlap, and the relationship to surrounding sections. Keep ordinary straight approach unless a specific exception has a musical purpose or explicit user request.
5. **Select optional effects.** Use the bank only after stating the effect's role and rhythmic anchors. Account for every affected note and line lifecycle.
6. **Implement and review within the existing workflow.** Use fresh context, dry-run, revision guards, undo, and save. Review the phrase with neighboring material in native motion and with the corresponding audio when evaluating musical fit. Follow existing sample-review requirements; an explicitly authorized full-length draft remains authorized at that scope.
7. **Record the result.** Update the song's existing project record with concrete decisions, review findings, and uncertainty. Apply established patterns autonomously where authorized; do not ask the user to decide every routine placement.

## Minimum phrase record

Extend the existing action account in the song's project records. Do not create a parallel chart state or a separate planning framework. Group repeated phrases under one shared account and describe only their differences when appropriate.

| Field | Required content |
| --- | --- |
| Range and musical basis | Concrete beats or times, audible grouping, rests, and the layer being represented. |
| Action and spatial structure | Hit order, simultaneous sets, contact gestures, candidate finger paths, and the progression, anchor, return, or other relationship. |
| Identity and development | What repeats, what changes, and the musical reason. An unchanged repeat is a valid answer. |
| Presentation and transition | Primary visual task, spacing, entry, exit, and any approaching notes or sustained contacts crossing the boundary. |
| Optional effect | None, or its role, affected carriers, musical anchors, and intended perceptual accents. |
| Review and uncertainty | What timing, geometry, native motion, audio alignment, and fingering checks establish; remaining aesthetic or playability assumptions. |

## Completion criteria

Before marking an authored passage ready for review, verify that:

- Its musical grouping and spatial/action structure are concrete and consistent with the actual notes.
- Recurring ideas retain a recognizable relationship, and variations have a reason beyond novelty.
- Entry, exit, contact occupancy, and plausible finger paths have been checked with neighboring material.
- Spacing, empty regions, and visual priority support reading the phrase.
- Optional effects have a supported role; background motion has explicit rhythmic anchors.
- Note visibility, carrier transitions, and hold tails have been reviewed over time, with no unintended timing, combo, or scoring changes.
- Remaining musical, aesthetic, or execution uncertainty is recorded accurately.

These checks establish disciplined design and review, not automatic artistic success. Use user feedback to refine specific choices and their conditions of applicability. Do not turn one approved example into a universal layout, tempo, animation, or emotion rule.
